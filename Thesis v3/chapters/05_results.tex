\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

\section{Local Projections}

This chapter reports the headline empirical result of the thesis and the
baseline specification grid that justifies the preferred fixed-effect
structure. \Cref{sec:res-main} presents the preferred-specification impulse
response of cumulative log returns to a forward-guidance path-factor
surprise, traced across $h = 0, 1, \dots, 60$ trading days after the FOMC
announcement, separately for High AI and Moderate AI firms relative to the
No AI omitted group. \Cref{sec:res-specgrid} shows that the long-horizon
component of the pattern survives the inclusion of sector\,$\times$\,event
fixed effects, that the magnitude is reduced by about a third when that
fixed-effect structure is added, and that adding shock\,$\times$\,controls
on top of the preferred fixed-effect structure leaves the key-horizon
coefficients within one standard error of the preferred-specification
estimates.

\section{Main result: differential path-shock response by AI exposure}
\label{sec:res-main}

\Cref{fig:preferred_path_irf} plots the local-projection impulse-response
estimates from the preferred specification, with firm fixed effects and
sector\,$\times$\,event fixed effects, no shock\,$\times$\,controls, and
two-way clustered standard errors by \texttt{firm\_id} and \texttt{event\_id}.
The two series trace, at every trading-day horizon
$h = 0, 1, \dots, 60$ after the FOMC announcement, the additional
cumulative log-return response of High AI and Moderate AI firms relative
to No AI firms in the same GICS sector on the same FOMC event, in response
to a $+25$ basis-point hawkish path-factor surprise. The shaded bands are
pointwise 95\% confidence intervals constructed under the scaling defined
in \cref{sec:emp-inference}. \Cref{tab:preferred_path_irf} reports the
scaled coefficients, two-way clustered standard errors, and 95\%
confidence intervals at the six key horizons $h \in \{0, 1, 5, 20, 40, 60\}$.

The active sample stacks \texttt{sample\_preferred} $= 1$, the date filter
\mbox{[2010-01-01, 2026-12-31]}, and the ticker-reuse correction
\texttt{ai\_score\_inherited} $= 0$, and contains 450 unique firms, 128
scheduled FOMC events, and 53{,}679 firm--event observations at $h = 0$;
attrition to $h = 60$ is mild, with 53{,}626 firm--event observations
remaining, so the panel is close to balanced across horizons.

The $+25$ basis-point benchmark is a reporting convention rather than a
claim about the typical surprise size; the in-sample distribution of
\texttt{path\_3f} is documented in the data chapter (see
\cref{sec:lp-data}). \texttt{path\_3f} is the path factor scaled into
basis points, signed so that a positive realisation corresponds to a
hawkish/tightening forward-guidance surprise (see \cref{sec:emp-shock}).
A negative scaled differential therefore means that the AI-exposed group
falls by more (or rises by less) than the No AI group following a hawkish
surprise.

At short and intermediate horizons the differentials are economically
small and the data do not reject zero. For High AI firms relative to No AI
firms, the scaled coefficient is $-0.63$ percentage points at the
announcement day ($h = 0$, SE $0.50$), $-0.25$ at $h = 1$ (SE $1.11$),
$-0.34$ at $h = 5$ (SE $0.90$), and $-0.60$ at $h = 20$ (SE $1.36$). Each
of the four pointwise 95\% confidence intervals contains zero. The
Moderate AI differential is small and mixed in sign at those four
horizons, with scaled coefficients of $+0.19$ at $h = 0$, $-0.24$ at $h = 1$,
$-0.44$ at $h = 5$, and $-1.15$ at $h = 20$; the 95\% confidence intervals
include zero in every case. The short- and intermediate-horizon point
estimates are therefore uniformly negative for High AI and mixed in sign
for Moderate AI at the shortest horizons; all corresponding confidence
intervals contain zero, and the chapter does not read them as evidence of
a non-zero differential at those horizons.

The horizon profile changes at the longer end of the post-event window.
For High AI firms relative to No AI firms, the scaled differential is
$-6.49$ percentage points at $h = 40$ (SE $2.11$; 95\% CI $[-10.62, -2.35]$)
and $-7.60$ percentage points at $h = 60$ (SE $2.53$; 95\% CI
$[-12.56, -2.63]$). At both horizons the pointwise 95\% confidence
interval excludes zero. The Moderate AI differential moves in the same
direction but is smaller and less precise: $-2.02$ percentage points at
$h = 40$ (SE $1.83$; 95\% CI $[-5.62, +1.57]$) and $-3.22$ percentage
points at $h = 60$ (SE $1.85$; 95\% CI $[-6.84, +0.41]$); the 95\%
confidence interval contains zero at every key horizon, though the
$h = 60$ upper bound is now close to zero. The pattern is therefore concentrated in the High AI tail of the
AI-exposure distribution rather than graded monotonically across the two
exposed groups; the empirical signal is not a smooth dose-response in
measured AI exposure but rather a tail-versus-rest pattern. The same
asymmetry between High AI and Moderate AI holds in each of the four
specifications reported in \cref{sec:res-specgrid}.

Four caveats discipline the reading of the long-horizon estimates.
First, the panel as constructed in \cref{ch:data} anchors
\texttt{cum\_ret\_h} at the trading day before the announcement and runs
over $h \in \{0, 1, \dots, 60\}$, so the chapter does not report a
pre-trend diagnostic at $h < 0$. Identification therefore rests on the
high-frequency nature of the path-factor surprise around the FOMC
announcement, as discussed in \cref{sec:emp-shock}, rather than on a
graphical pre-trend test; a clean pre-event null at $h < 0$ would in any
case only rule out anticipated leakage of the path-factor surprise, not
confounding that is symmetric around the announcement. Second, the 95\%
confidence intervals reported in \cref{tab:preferred_path_irf} and in
\cref{fig:preferred_path_irf} are pointwise rather than joint across
horizons; a band-wise correction would widen the intervals at the
$h = 40$ and $h = 60$ horizons that drive the result. Third, a
60-trading-day horizon spans roughly three calendar months, during which
other macroeconomic releases, including subsequent FOMC meetings,
intervene; the local-projection coefficient at long horizons is still a
valid projection but its interpretation as the response to \emph{this}
surprise weakens with $h$. Fourth, the inference properties of local
projections at longer horizons have been documented to degrade in
persistent settings \citep{olea2024lp}; the inference-sensitivity check
in \cref{sec:rob-inference} re-estimates the key-horizon coefficients
under alternative cluster choices and reports the change. With those
caveats in mind, the chapter takes as its headline reading that the data
are consistent with a delayed, long-horizon differential return response
for High AI firms relative to No AI firms in the same sector on the same
FOMC event, building from a precisely-estimated zero at impact to roughly
$-6$ to $-8$ percentage points by $h \in \{40, 60\}$ per $+25$
basis-point hawkish path-factor surprise; the Moderate AI differential
is not statistically distinguishable from zero at any of the six key
horizons.

The empirical strategy in \cref{ch:empirical-strategy} identifies a within-sector,
within-event differential and does not separate the channels through
which the differential is generated. The delayed, long-horizon profile
observed here is consistent with channels in which AI-exposed firms are
more sensitive to forward-guidance surprises than non-exposed firms in
the same sector on the same FOMC date, but the design cannot adjudicate
between competing mechanisms (e.g.\ cash-flow duration, intangible
capital, growth-firm rate sensitivity, or other firm-level channels
proposed in the literature reviewed in \cref{ch:literature}); the
discussion in \cref{ch:conclusion} returns to this. The chapter does not
make a level claim about the unconditional response of either AI group:
under the preferred specification the sector\,$\times$\,event fixed
effect absorbs every component of the response that is common to the
sector at the event, so $\hat\beta_h^H$ and $\hat\beta_h^M$ identify the
\emph{within-cell differential} rather than the level. Pooled estimates
from Spec~1 of the specification grid, in which the path factor enters as
a stand-alone regressor, are reported in \cref{tab:baseline_spec_grid_key}
and are discussed in \cref{sec:res-specgrid}.

Alternative shocks, alternative outcomes, alternative AI
classifications, and alternative sample windows are reported in
\cref{sec:rob-altshocks,sec:rob-altoutcomes,sec:rob-altai,sec:rob-samplewindows};
the inference-sensitivity check is reported in
\cref{sec:rob-inference}. Heterogeneity in the differential response
by financial leverage is examined in \cref{sec:res-heterogeneity};
complementary mechanism-oriented evidence on second-moment outcomes
and on the symmetry of the response to tightening and easing
surprises is reported in
\cref{app:rob-volatility,app:rob-asymmetry}.
 

\section{Baseline specification grid}
\label{sec:res-specgrid}

The preferred specification combines firm fixed effects with
sector\,$\times$\,event fixed effects and no shock\,$\times$\,controls.
\Cref{fig:baseline_spec_grid} reports the four-panel baseline
specification grid for \texttt{path\_3f} on the same active sample,
estimating the same two interactions $\beta_h^H$ and $\beta_h^M$ at every
trading-day horizon under four nested fixed-effect and control choices:
firm fixed effects only (Spec~1); firm fixed effects with
shock\,$\times$\,controls (Spec~2); firm fixed effects with
sector\,$\times$\,event fixed effects (Spec~3, the preferred specification);
and firm fixed effects with sector\,$\times$\,event fixed effects and
shock\,$\times$\,controls (Spec~4). The shock\,$\times$\,controls vector
contains \texttt{path\_x\_logmktcap}, \texttt{path\_x\_beta}, and
\texttt{path\_x\_ivol}, constructed as in \cref{sec:emp-lp}; in Spec~1
the path factor enters as a stand-alone regressor as well as in the two
interactions, while in Spec~3 and Spec~4 the stand-alone path factor is
absorbed by the sector\,$\times$\,event fixed effects and is dropped.
\Cref{tab:baseline_spec_grid_key} reports the scaled coefficients and
clustered standard errors at the six key horizons.

The long-horizon High AI differential is present in all four
specifications and largest in Spec~1, which has the weakest controls for
event-time variation. At $h = 60$, the scaled High AI differential is
$-11.85$ percentage points under Spec~1, $-8.31$ under Spec~2, $-7.60$
under Spec~3, and $-5.35$ under Spec~4. At $h = 40$ the same monotone
pattern holds: $-8.19$ in Spec~1, $-6.73$ in Spec~2, $-6.49$ in Spec~3,
and $-5.19$ in Spec~4. Moving from Spec~1 to Spec~3 reduces the
long-horizon High AI differential by roughly a third in absolute size at
$h = 60$ (from $-11.85$ to $-7.60$ percentage points, or about 36\%) and
by about a fifth at $h = 40$ (from $-8.19$ to $-6.49$, or about 21\%). That reduction is consistent with
sector\,$\times$\,event fixed effects absorbing common sector-level
responses to FOMC announcements that would otherwise load partly on the
High AI indicator: roughly a third of the long-horizon Spec~1 High AI
differential at $h = 60$ is sector-level co-movement rather than
within-sector heterogeneity. The remaining two thirds is the
within-sector, within-event differential that the preferred specification
isolates.

Comparing Spec~3 and Spec~4 isolates the role of shock\,$\times$\,controls
conditional on the preferred fixed-effect structure. % TODO: Insert exact standard-error-distance comparison if required.
At both long
horizons, the High AI differential under Spec~4 is somewhat smaller in
absolute size than under Spec~3 ($-5.19$ vs.\ $-6.49$ at $h = 40$; $-5.35$
vs.\ $-7.60$ at $h = 60$). Adding shock\,$\times$\,controls on top of the
preferred fixed-effect structure therefore attenuates the long-horizon
High AI differential, but does not overturn its negative sign. The Moderate AI differential is
small and noisy at every horizon in every specification and is
statistically indistinguishable from zero at all six key horizons under
all four specifications.

The Spec~1 estimates also provide the only pooled-LP view of the path
factor that the design produces, since in Spec~3 and Spec~4 the
stand-alone path factor is absorbed by the sector\,$\times$\,event fixed
effects. Reading the Spec~1 High AI differential together with the
absorbed stand-alone path-factor coefficient would in principle give the
unconditional path-factor response of the High AI group; that
decomposition is not the object of interest of the chapter and is not
reported here, but the Spec~1 column of
\cref{tab:baseline_spec_grid_key} preserves the building blocks for a
reader who wants to construct it.

The takeaway from \cref{fig:baseline_spec_grid} and
\cref{tab:baseline_spec_grid_key} is twofold. The long-horizon High AI
differential survives the inclusion of sector\,$\times$\,event fixed
effects and is reduced by roughly a third in absolute size by that
inclusion, indicating that sector-level co-movement with the path-factor
shock accounts for a meaningful but not dominant fraction of the Spec~1
estimate. Conditional on sector\,$\times$\,event fixed effects, adding
shock\,$\times$\,controls moves the long-horizon High AI differential by
less than one standard error. The chapter uses Spec~3 as the preferred
specification, in line with the supervisor instruction recorded in
\cref{ch:empirical-strategy}, and treats Spec~4 as a robustness variant.

\section{Heterogeneity: financial leverage}
\label{sec:res-heterogeneity}
 
The preferred specification identifies a within-sector, within-event
differential by AI exposure but does not separate the channels
through which the differential is generated. The most prominent
firm-level heterogeneity in the monetary-transmission literature is
financial leverage: \citet{ottonelloWinberry2020} and a substantial
related literature show that high-leverage firms respond more
strongly to contractionary monetary surprises than low-leverage
firms. If AI-exposed firms are also high-leverage firms, the
differential documented in \cref{sec:res-main} could in part reflect
that channel rather than an AI dimension distinct from leverage.
This section asks directly whether the AI differential survives
controlling for an interaction between the path shock and firm
leverage, and whether the leverage gap inside the AI groups is
large enough to be a quantitatively important driver of the
headline.
 
\paragraph{Specification and sample.} The preferred specification is
extended with a within-sector-event median split of the lagged
winsorised debt-to-assets ratio, $\texttt{debt\_assets\_lag\_w}$,
producing a binary indicator $\texttt{high\_debt\_sector}$ that
flags firms above the sector-event median at $h = 0$. The regression
adds three interactions to the preferred two:
$\texttt{path\_x\_hdebt} = \texttt{path\_3f} \times
\texttt{high\_debt\_sector}$, $\texttt{path\_x\_high\_hdebt} =
\texttt{path\_x\_high} \times \texttt{high\_debt\_sector}$, and
$\texttt{path\_x\_mod\_hdebt} = \texttt{path\_x\_mod} \times
\texttt{high\_debt\_sector}$. Fixed effects, clustering, and the
$\times 25 \times 100$ rescaling are unchanged. The sample is
restricted to firm-event observations with non-missing lagged debt
data ($\texttt{sample\_debt} == 1$), yielding $37{,}092$ observations
at $h = 0$ after \texttt{reghdfe} drops $22$ singletons. The
sector-event median split is coarser than firm-level continuous
leverage, so the analysis is qualitatively but not quantitatively
comparable to \citet{ottonelloWinberry2020}; a continuous-leverage
specification is reported below.
 
A descriptive fact is worth establishing first. The mean winsorised
debt-to-assets ratio is $0.26$ in the No AI group, $0.22$ in
Moderate AI, and $0.19$ in High AI. AI-exposed firms in the active
sample are therefore on average \emph{less} indebted than non-exposed
firms in their sector. If financial leverage and the AI dimension
moved in the same direction, the headline differential could
mechanically reflect a credit channel; the descriptive ordering goes
the other way.
 
\paragraph{The no-AI leverage gap.} The directly comparable quantity
is the high-debt-minus-low-debt cumulative-return gap, identified
within sector-event cells in each AI group. In the No AI baseline
this gap is negative at every key horizon and statistically
distinguishable from zero at $h = 20$ ($-1.64$~pp, s.e.~$0.80$,
$95\%$ CI $[-3.20, -0.08]$). The gap grows in absolute value through
$h = 40$ ($-3.01$~pp, s.e.~$1.61$, CI just contains zero) and
partially recovers thereafter. The sign and shape are qualitatively
consistent with the credit channel \citep{ottonelloWinberry2020}:
among firms with no AI exposure, high-leverage firms respond more
negatively than low-leverage firms in the same sector-event cell.
 
\paragraph{Leverage gaps inside the AI groups.} The same gap inside
each AI group is statistically indistinguishable from zero at every
key horizon. At $h = 20$, the gap is $-0.84$~pp (s.e.~$2.04$,
CI $[-4.84, 3.17]$) in the High AI group and $-0.26$~pp
(s.e.~$1.46$, CI $[-3.11, 2.60]$) in the Moderate AI group; the
within-AI gaps remain similarly imprecise at longer horizons. The
within-AI standard errors are substantially wider than the No AI
baseline because the two AI groups together account for roughly
$30\%$ of the sample, but the point estimates are also smaller in
absolute value than the No AI baseline at every horizon where the
baseline is itself non-trivial.
 
\paragraph{The formal amplification test.} The cleanest statement of
the credit-channel-as-driver hypothesis is that the leverage gap
inside the AI groups is \emph{larger} than the leverage gap in the
No AI baseline. The regression delivers this directly as the
amplification coefficients $\texttt{path\_x\_high\_hdebt}$ and
$\texttt{path\_x\_mod\_hdebt}$: under the null of no amplification,
both should be zero. \Cref{tab:debt_amplification_key} reports the
two coefficients at the seven key horizons together with the no-AI
baseline gap for context. Neither amplification coefficient is
statistically distinguishable from zero at any key horizon. Point
estimates are mostly \emph{positive} --- meaning the leverage gap is
smaller (less negative) inside the AI groups than in the No AI
baseline, the opposite of amplification. At $h = 40$ the High AI
amplification is $+2.66$~pp (s.e.~$3.93$, CI $[-5.04, 10.36]$) and
the Moderate AI amplification is $+1.90$~pp (s.e.~$2.35$, CI
$[-2.70, 6.50]$). The data are imprecise on this margin and cannot
rule out leverage amplification of moderate magnitude, but they
also cannot detect any.

\paragraph{Continuous-debt specification.} Replacing the sector-event
median split with a continuous sector-standardised debt-to-assets
score, $\texttt{debt\_z\_sector}$, in interaction with the AI groups
gives the same qualitative reading. The amplification coefficients
$\texttt{path\_x\_high\_debtz}$ and $\texttt{path\_x\_mod\_debtz}$
are indistinguishable from zero at every key horizon, with point
estimates of mixed sign and small absolute magnitude relative to
their standard errors. At $h = 40$, the High AI amplification per
one-standard-deviation increase in sector-standardised leverage is
$+0.85$~pp (s.e.~$1.94$); the Moderate AI counterpart is $+0.80$~pp
(s.e.~$1.91$). Full continuous-debt estimates appear in
\cref{app:additional-results}.
 
\paragraph{Summary.} The leverage dimension of firm heterogeneity is
present in the data --- the No AI baseline leverage gap is
detectable and sign-consistent with the credit channel --- but it
does not extend to the AI groups in a way that would explain the
headline differential. Three points stand: (i) High AI firms are on
average less indebted than No AI firms, in the wrong direction for
leverage to mechanically generate the headline; (ii) the leverage
gap inside the AI groups is indistinguishable from zero at every key
horizon, while the no-AI baseline gap is detectable; and (iii) the
formal amplification test fails to reject the null at every key
horizon, with point estimates running in the opposite direction.
This section cannot positively establish what is driving the AI
differential --- it is silent on that mechanism --- but it can rule
out one salient channel: the AI--path differential identified in
\cref{sec:res-main} is not explained by financial-leverage
heterogeneity across AI groups.


\section{Bubbles}
 
This chapter reports the full-sample PSY analysis of the twenty-eight monthly series defined in \cref{subsec:bubble-test-variables}, estimated under the recursive specification of \cref{subsec:bubble-specification} and the wild-multiplier bootstrap of \cref{subsec:bubble-critical-values}. The results are organised in the same order as the design. \Cref{sec:results-headline} reports the GSADF statistics for the sixteen headline series and compares the ordering across AI-exposure groups. \Cref{sec:results-episodes} reports the date-stamped episodes implied by the rule in \cref{subsec:bubble-date-stamping}. \Cref{sec:results-concentration} turns to the twelve concentration-basket series and asks whether the strongest evidence of explosiveness is concentrated among a small number of AI-related market leaders. The lag-order, log-specification and wild-bootstrap-multiplier robustness exercises previewed in \cref{subsec:bubble-robustness-design} are deferred to \cref{ch:robustness}; all critical values reported below use the corrected wild multiplier.
 
Two conventions discipline the interpretation. First, every series is reported under both value- and equal-weighting. The gap between the two is read as the breadth diagnostic introduced in \cref{subsec:bubble-overview}: value-weighted evidence that is much stronger than equal-weighted evidence points to concentration among large firms, while similar evidence under both weightings indicates broader participation across the tested group. Second, the substantive interpretation never rests on a single rejection. Absolute indices, ratio series and date-stamped episodes are read jointly, as required by the identification limits in \cref{subsec:bubble-design-limitations}. A GSADF rejection is evidence of explosive price dynamics; it is not, by itself, evidence that prices exceed fundamentals.

\section{Full-sample GSADF: AI groups and broad-market reference}
\label{sec:results-headline}
 
\Cref{tab:gsadf-headline} reports the full-sample GSADF statistic for the sixteen headline series. Fifteen of the sixteen reject the unit-root-with-drift null at the 95\,\% bootstrap critical value or above, and six reject at the 99\,\% level; the remaining series, the value-weighted No AI / S\&P~500 diagnostic, rejects only at 90\,\%. Explosive dynamics are therefore not confined to the AI-exposed cohort. The headline result is a broad post-2010 tendency toward explosive behaviour, with the strongest statistics appearing in the High-AI series and in the High-AI relative ratios.
 
Within the absolute-index block, the ordering is consistent with AI exposure. The High AI index produces a GSADF statistic of $4.04$ under value-weighting and $4.25$ under equal-weighting, in both cases above the 99\,\% critical value (\num{2.57} and \num{2.62}, respectively). The Moderate AI index produces $2.35$ and $2.24$, each above the 95\,\% critical value but well below the High AI statistic. The No AI index produces the smallest values among the AI groups ($1.86$ and $1.97$), exceeding 95\,\% but not 99\,\%. The S\&P~500 benchmark sits between the No AI and Moderate AI indices at $2.17$ under both weightings. Because all four absolute indices reject at the 95\,\% level, the level evidence should mainly be read as a diagnostic: the test detects explosive dynamics across the broader equity market, not only inside the AI-exposed portfolios.
 
The ratio series provide the sharper cross-sectional evidence. The High AI / No AI ratio --- the primary identification in \cref{subsec:bubble-test-variables} --- rejects at the 99\,\% level under both weightings ($3.15$ and $3.19$, against 99\,\% critical values of $2.46$ and $2.35$). The High AI / S\&P~500 ratio also rejects at the 99\,\% level ($3.41$ value-weighted, $2.85$ equal-weighted). By contrast, the Moderate AI / S\&P~500 ratio rejects only at the 95\,\% level under both weightings ($1.38$ and $1.57$), and the No AI / S\&P~500 placebo-style diagnostic rejects at the 90\,\% level under value-weighting and at the 95\,\% level under equal-weighting ($1.45$ and $1.63$). The placebo-style diagnostic is therefore not cleanly inside the null, which weakens a narrow AI-only interpretation. At the same time, the High AI ratios are much larger than the No AI / S\&P~500 ratio, so the cross-sectional ordering remains informative.
 
The breadth diagnostic points in the same direction. The High AI absolute index, the High AI / No AI ratio and the High AI / S\&P~500 ratio reject at the 99\,\% level under both value- and equal-weighting. The High-AI signal is therefore not mechanically produced by value-weighting alone; it remains visible when each firm in the High-AI cohort receives equal weight. The concentration analysis below qualifies this statement by showing that the strongest statistics are still concentrated among mega-cap leaders, but the headline High-AI result itself is not eliminated by equal-weighting.

\section{Date-stamped explosive episodes}
\label{sec:results-episodes}
 
\Cref{fig:groups-vw-fullsample} reports the shaded-index plots for the eight headline series under value-weighting, with graded shading at the 90\,\%, 95\,\% and 99\,\% bootstrap critical values as described in \cref{subsec:bubble-date-stamping}. \Cref{tab:episodes-headline} lists the dated episodes for the High-AI series under value-weighting; the equal-weighted counterpart and the dated episodes for the Moderate~AI, No~AI and S\&P~500 series are deferred to \cref{app:episodes-full}.

The date-stamping adds an important qualification to the full-sample GSADF results. First, the High AI absolute index is flagged in four value-weighted episodes: July~2017--September~2018 ($15$ months, peak BSADF $3.27$ in August~2018), June~2020--January~2022 ($20$ months, peak $4.04$ in August~2021), February~2024--February~2025 ($13$ months, peak $2.61$ in June~2024), and May~2025--February~2026 ($10$ months, peak $3.42$ in October~2025). Under equal-weighting, the two pre-ChatGPT episodes are similarly placed (April~2017--September~2018 and November~2020--March~2022), but the late-sample re-emergence is absent. The post-2022 High-AI level episodes are therefore mainly a value-weighted phenomenon, while the 2017--18 and 2020--21 episodes are broad enough within the High-AI cohort to survive equal-weighting.
 
Second, the differential High AI / No AI series is dated before the ChatGPT release. Under value-weighting, the primary ratio is flagged in February--June~2018, March~2020--February~2021, and June--November~2021. Under equal-weighting, the same basic timing recurs in February~2020--April~2021 and June--December~2021. These five differential episodes all end before November~2022. The High AI / S\&P~500 ratio shows a similar pre-ChatGPT pattern: the value-weighted ratio is dated in 2018, 2019, 2020--21 and 2021, while the equal-weighted ratio compresses much of this behaviour into a single January~2017--December~2021 window. The implication is not that AI is irrelevant after 2022. Rather, in this full-sample design the post-ChatGPT evidence appears more clearly in absolute High-AI levels and in concentration baskets than in the broad High AI / No AI differential ratio.
 
Third, the broad-market reference series also matter for interpretation. The No AI absolute index rejects in the full-sample table, but under value-weighting it has no 95\,\% episode that survives the five-month duration filter; its rejection is generated by shorter threshold crossings. Under equal-weighting, the No AI index has a single five-month episode between April and August~2021. The S\&P~500 benchmark is flagged in two episodes under both weightings: April--August~2021 and July~2025--February~2026. The late-sample S\&P~500 episode is mirrored in the value-weighted High-AI index, but not in the equal-weighted High-AI index or in the broad High AI / No AI ratio. This matters because group membership is fixed using the pre-ChatGPT classification: firms that became AI-relevant after the classification date remain in whichever group held them at that date, rather than being reallocated into the High-AI cohort. These episodes therefore reinforce the main identification caution: the sample contains broad-market explosiveness, so the AI interpretation has to be based on the relative strength, timing and concentration of the AI-related series rather than on the mere existence of a GSADF rejection.

\section{Concentration baskets}
\label{sec:results-concentration}
 
\Cref{tab:gsadf-topn} reports the GSADF statistics for the three concentration baskets defined in \cref{subsec:bubble-test-variables}: Top-5, Top-decile and Mag-7. Each basket is divided by the No-AI and High-AI denominators and tested under both value- and equal-weighting. All twelve concentration ratios reject at the 90\,\% level, nine reject at the 95\,\% level, and seven reject at the 99\,\% level. The evidence is therefore strongest for the concentration ratios relative to No AI and for the Mag-7 ratios; it is weaker when the disclosure-based Top-5 and Top-decile baskets are compared with the broader High-AI portfolio. \Cref{fig:topn-vw-fullsample} shows the corresponding shaded-ratio plots under value-weighting.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{outputs/figures/main/ai_topN_vw_fullsample.png}
\caption{Shaded-ratio plot of the concentration baskets, value-weighted, full sample.}
\label{fig:topn-vw-fullsample}
\caption*{\footnotesize \textit{Notes.} Ratios of the Top-5, Top-decile and Mag-7 baskets to the No-AI and High-AI denominators, under value-weighting. Shading and reference line as in \cref{fig:groups-vw-fullsample}. The equal-weighted counterpart is reported in \cref{app:psy-ew}.}
\end{figure}
 
The Mag-7 / No AI series produces the largest GSADF statistics in the analysis: $5.20$ under value-weighting and $6.72$ under equal-weighting, against 99\,\% critical values of $2.39$ and $2.45$, respectively. The dated episodes place the largest contiguous window between March~2020 and February~2021 under value-weighting, and between February~2020 and February~2021 under equal-weighting, with peak BSADF statistics of $5.20$ and $6.72$ in August~2020. Both weightings also show a second June--December~2021 episode. The Mag-7 / High AI ratio remains significant at the 99\,\% level under both weightings, with GSADF statistics of $3.33$ under value-weighting and $4.49$ under equal-weighting. Its dated episodes are especially informative: the equal-weighted series is flagged in June--October~2020, October~2024--February~2025, and May~2025--January~2026, while the value-weighted series rejects in the full-sample statistic but has no 95\,\% episode that survives the duration filter. The seven mega-cap names are therefore explosive not only relative to No AI firms but also relative to the broader High-AI portfolio, with the post-2022 Mag-7 / High AI episodes appearing most clearly under equal-weighting. Because the equal-weighted Mag-7 ratios are larger than their value-weighted counterparts, the Mag-7 result is not driven solely by the largest one or two firms in the basket. This is consistent with \citet{baselePhillipsShi2025}, who find explosive behaviour in six of the seven Mag-7 stocks over a comparable sample.
 
The disclosure-based Top-5 and Top-decile baskets produce smaller statistics, but they preserve the same broad ordering. The Top-5 / No AI ratio rejects at the 99\,\% level under both weightings ($4.09$ value-weighted and $2.62$ equal-weighted). Under value-weighting, its dated episodes occur in January--October~2020, June--December~2021, January--July~2024 and June--October~2025; under equal-weighting, only a short January--May~2018 episode survives the duration filter. The Top-decile / No AI ratio rejects at the 99\,\% level under value-weighting and at the 95\,\% level under equal-weighting ($3.35$ and $2.04$), but only the value-weighted series has dated 95\,\% episodes, both in 2020--21. These patterns imply that disclosure-based concentration is present, but it is more value-weighted and less persistent than the Mag-7 concentration signal.
 
The ratios to High AI add a useful qualification. Top-5 / High AI rejects at the 95\,\% level under value-weighting but only at 90\,\% under equal-weighting; Top-decile / High AI rejects only at 90\,\% under both weightings. Thus, the top disclosure-ranked firms are clearly explosive relative to No AI firms, but much less clearly explosive relative to the broader High-AI group. By contrast, Mag-7 / High AI remains large and significant under both weightings. This distinction matters for the interpretation: the strongest concentration evidence is not simply that the firms with the highest pre-2023 AI keyword scores dominate the High-AI portfolio, but that the market's mega-cap AI narrative stocks display exceptional relative explosiveness.
 
Taken together, the concentration block sharpens the headline result. The broad High AI / No AI ratio rejects strongly, but its dated episodes are mainly pre-ChatGPT. The post-2022 signal appears more clearly in value-weighted High-AI levels and in the Top-5 and Mag-7 concentration baskets. The evidence is therefore consistent with bubble-like dynamics around AI-exposed and AI-associated firms, but it is not a clean post-2022 AI-bubble verdict. It points instead to a combination of long-running technology-sector outperformance, broad-market explosiveness and a particularly strong concentration component among mega-cap AI leaders.
