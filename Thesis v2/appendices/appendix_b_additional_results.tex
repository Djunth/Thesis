\section{BSADF-vs-critical-value figures}
\label{app:psy-bsadf-cv}
 
The shaded-index figures in \cref{ch:results} and \cref{app:psy-ew} mark whether the BSADF sequence exceeds the bootstrap critical values, but suppress the magnitude of any exceedance. This appendix reports the canonical Phillips--Shi monitoring view: for each series, the BSADF statistic sequence is plotted in levels against a flat 95\,\% bootstrap critical value (red dashed), with the 90\,\% and 99\,\% values shown as grey dotted reference lines. The same critical values are used as in the main-text tables and figures; the figures here therefore preserve the information in the shading at the cost of being more cluttered. They are useful for reading off the magnitude of the BSADF exceedance during the dated episodes of \cref{sec:results-episodes,sec:results-concentration} --- for example, the August~2020 spike in the Mag-7 / No AI series exceeds the 95\,\% critical value by a wider margin than any other point in the analysis, and is visible directly in the bottom-left panel of \cref{fig:topn-vw-bsadf-cv}.
 
A small interpretive note. The flat critical value reflects the wild-bootstrap monitoring value calibrated to the control window $T_b$ described in \cref{subsec:bubble-critical-values}, not the time-varying critical-value sequence of \citet[Section~5]{phillipsShiYu2015a}. As warned in \cref{subsec:bubble-date-stamping}, this is the standard usage of \texttt{psymonitor} and is somewhat permissive in dating the later, larger-window portion of the sample; the BSADF-vs-CV figures should therefore be read for magnitude rather than as a precise alternative date-stamping device.

\section{Continuous AI exposure}
\label{app:rob-altai-continuous}

The headline result in \cref{ch:results} and the discrete-classification
check in \cref{sec:rob-altai} both rely on the three-way grouping
\{\texttt{high\_ai}, \texttt{moderate\_ai}, \texttt{no\_ai}\}.
A complementary check replaces the discrete groupings entirely with
a continuous standardised log AI score, \texttt{logAIz}, interacted
with \texttt{path\_3f}. The estimating equation collapses to a single
interaction term:
\begin{equation*}
    \mathtt{cum\_ret\_h}_{i,e,h}
    \;=\; \alpha_i + \gamma_{s(i)\times e}
    \;+\; \beta_h\,\mathtt{path\_x\_logAIz}_{i,e}
    \;+\; \varepsilon_{i,e,h},
\end{equation*}
where \texttt{logAIz} is approximately mean zero and unit variance on
the active sample (mean $0.006$, SD $1.007$). The scaled coefficient
$\hat\beta \times 25 \times 100$ is interpreted as the differential
cumulative-return response in percentage points to a $+25$ bp path
surprise per one-standard-deviation increase in \texttt{logAIz}.

Point estimates are uniformly negative at horizons $h \geq 15$ and
grow in absolute value with the horizon, reaching $-3.50$~pp per SD
at $h = 60$. The shape closely tracks the High AI differential under
the discrete specification: roughly flat through $h = 20$, then a
steady decline. Under the preferred two-way clustering by firm and
event, however, the standard-error estimator collapses at a
substantial fraction of horizons --- including two of the seven key
horizons ($h = 20$, $h = 40$) --- returning a numerical value of
zero that the do-file replaces with missing. The collapse reflects
the limited between-cluster variation in \texttt{logAIz} relative to
the discrete-group interactions and is a known property of the
cluster-robust variance estimator rather than a regression bug; it
is not specific to any one horizon.

Sensitivity to the clustering choice resolves the inference issue.
Under firm-only clustering, event-only clustering, or
heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, the standard error is
well defined at every horizon and the point estimates are unchanged
by construction. Under any of these three alternative inference
schemes the long-horizon coefficient is distinguishable from zero at
the $5\%$ level: at $h = 60$, for example, the firm-clustered
standard error is $1.15$~pp, giving a $95\%$ confidence interval of
$[-5.75, -1.26]$.

The continuous-exposure evidence is therefore qualitatively

the well-known small-cluster issue and is reported with the
appropriate caveat.

\section{Volatility outcomes}
\label{app:rob-volatility}

\Cref{sec:rob-altoutcomes} addresses whether the headline mean
differential survives stripping out market-wide returns; the answer is
yes. A distinct second-moment question is whether the path shock
generates a differential \emph{volatility} response across AI groups,
in addition to or independent of the mean response. The distinction
matters for interpretation: a cash-flow- or discount-rate-channel
response shifts expected payoffs and would not necessarily raise
return dispersion, while an information-content interpretation
(under which the path shock conveys more news about the fundamentals
of AI-exposed firms than other firms) would predict differential
volatility alongside the differential mean. The check is reported
here in the appendix rather than as part of the main robustness
chapter because a null on volatility is not a threat to the
headline mean claim --- the result is best read as complementary
evidence about mechanism rather than as defensive evidence about
the result itself.

\paragraph{Specification.} The preferred specification is unchanged
except for the outcome. Two volatility measures are used:
\texttt{avg\_abs\_ret\_h}, the average absolute daily log return over
the post-event window of $h+1$ trading days (event day inclusive),
and \texttt{realized\_vol\_h}, the square root of the sum of squared
daily log returns over the same window. Neither outcome is
annualised, demeaned, or winsorised; both are constructed in the
master analysis panel in fractional units. Coefficients are rescaled
to a $+25$ bp path-shock surprise as in the preferred specification,
with the $\times 100$ factor converting fractional units to
percentage points. The active sample, fixed effects, clustering, and
AI grouping are identical to the preferred specification;
volatility-outcome sample sizes coincide with the headline regression
sample at every key horizon.

\Cref{tab:volatility_key} reports the High AI and Moderate AI
interaction coefficients at the seven key horizons under both
volatility outcomes. \Cref{fig:volatility_irf} plots the
corresponding impulse responses across $h = 0,\ldots,60$.

The High AI volatility response is statistically indistinguishable
from zero at every key horizon under both measures. Under
\texttt{realized\_vol\_h}, the High AI differential at $h = 60$ is
$+0.72$~pp (s.e.~$0.84$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.92, +2.35]$); under
\texttt{avg\_abs\_ret\_h} it is $+0.10$~pp (s.e.~$0.07$, CI $[-0.05,
+0.24]$). Point estimates are positive at every key horizon under
\texttt{avg\_abs\_ret\_h} and at all key horizons except $h = 5$
under \texttt{realized\_vol\_h}, broadly consistent with the
direction of a volatility increase, but the data do not support the
claim that the path shock generates a detectable differential
second-moment response for the firms that exhibit the largest mean
response in \cref{ch:results}.

The Moderate AI volatility response shows a different pattern, but
the signal is confined to a single horizon and measure. Under
\texttt{realized\_vol\_h}, the Moderate AI differential is positive
at every medium horizon and is statistically distinguishable from
zero only at $h = 30$ ($+1.22$~pp, s.e.~$0.35$, CI $[+0.54, +1.90]$);
at $h = 20$ the differential is positive but imprecise ($+0.43$~pp,
s.e.~$0.33$, CI $[-0.21, +1.07]$), with the $95\%$ CI containing
zero. Under \texttt{avg\_abs\_ret\_h} the $h = 20$ differential is
small and indistinguishable from zero ($+0.07$~pp, s.e.~$0.04$, CI
$[-0.01, +0.15]$). The signal attenuates at the longest horizons
($h = 50$ and $h = 60$) where the CIs contain zero under both
measures.

\paragraph{Reading.} The volatility check produces a qualitatively
distinct picture from the mean response. The path shock is
associated with higher return volatility for Moderate AI firms at a
single medium horizon ($h = 30$ under \texttt{realized\_vol\_h}) by
a modest amount, but does not produce a detectable differential
volatility response for High AI firms --- the very group that
exhibits the largest \emph{mean} response at long horizons. The
absence of a detectable differential volatility response in the
High AI group is consistent with the headline mean differential
reflecting a shift in expected payoffs rather than a shift in
payoff dispersion: a cash-flow- or discount-rate-channel
interpretation predicts mean movement without necessarily implying
variance movement, while an information-content interpretation
under which the path shock conveys more news about AI-exposed firms
than other firms would predict differential variance movement
alongside the mean. The data are noisy enough that the
information-content interpretation cannot be ruled out, but the
volatility evidence is not on its side. The medium-horizon Moderate
AI signal does not have a clean mean-response counterpart to anchor
against and cannot exclude that the pattern reflects statistical
chance across the multiple horizons and outcomes considered.

The volatility outcomes \texttt{avg\_abs\_ret\_h} and
\texttt{realized\_vol\_h} are constructed in the master Python
analysis-panel notebook \texttt{00\_build\_final\_lp\_analysis\_panel.ipynb},
not in the Stata pipeline that builds every other variable used in
the thesis. The construction is straightforward (a non-annualised
mean-absolute-deviation and a non-annualised realized volatility on
daily log returns) but the pipeline split is a minor
reproducibility note.

\section{Symmetry of the response to tightening and easing surprises}
\label{app:rob-asymmetry}

The preferred specification treats \texttt{path\_3f} as a linear
regressor and pools tightening and easing surprises. A natural
follow-up is whether the long-horizon differential response of High
AI firms is symmetric across the two directions of the surprise.
Under a textbook discount-rate channel, a $+25$ bp tightening
surprise should reduce long-horizon cumulative returns of High AI
firms by the same magnitude as a $-25$ bp easing surprise raises
them. Departures from symmetry would suggest a non-linear mechanism,
for example differential leverage effects, asymmetric attention to
policy surprises, or convex responses to uncertainty. The check is
reported here in the appendix rather than as part of the main
robustness chapter because it speaks to the mechanism (linearity of
the response in the size and direction of the shock) rather than to
the existence of the headline differential.

This appendix section re-estimates the preferred specification with
the path factor decomposed by sign,
$\texttt{path\_tight} = \texttt{path\_3f}$ if $\texttt{path\_3f} > 0$
and zero otherwise, $\texttt{path\_ease} = \texttt{path\_3f}$ if
$\texttt{path\_3f} < 0$ and zero otherwise. By construction
$\texttt{path\_tight} + \texttt{path\_ease} = \texttt{path\_3f}$
event by event. The estimating equation replaces the two preferred
interactions with four, one for each (direction $\times$ AI group)
combination. The active sample splits into $69$ tightening events
and $57$ easing events of the $128$ scheduled FOMC events. Fixed
effects, clustering, and the $\times 25 \times 100$ rescaling are
unchanged from the preferred specification.

\Cref{tab:asymmetry_key} reports the four interaction coefficients
at the seven key horizons together with the per-group symmetry
test. \Cref{fig:asymmetry} plots the corresponding impulse
responses over $h = 0,\ldots,60$.

The High AI point estimates are pattern-consistent with a symmetric
response. The High AI coefficient on \texttt{path\_tight} is
negative at the longer horizons, reaching $-5.77$~pp at $h = 40$
and $-6.65$~pp at $h = 60$. The coefficient on \texttt{path\_ease}
is correspondingly positive at the longer horizons --- meaning
that the easing surprise (a negative regressor) raises cumulative
returns of High AI firms --- with values of $+7.00$~pp at
$h = 40$, $+10.26$~pp at $h = 50$, and $+8.28$~pp at $h = 60$.
The implied magnitudes from the two directions are comparable in
absolute value. The per-horizon symmetry test for the High AI
interaction, H$_0$:
$\beta_{\text{tight}}^{H} + \beta_{\text{ease}}^{H} = 0$, fails to
reject at every key horizon, with $p$-values ranging from $0.33$
(at $h = 50$) to $0.83$ (at $h = 60$). The data are therefore consistent with the High AI response
being symmetric across the two directions of the surprise.

The Moderate AI estimates show a different and weaker pattern. Both
the tightening and easing interactions are negative at every key
horizon, implying that Moderate AI firms underperform under either
direction of the path surprise. The Moderate AI symmetry test
rejects symmetry at the $10\%$ level at the shorter horizons ($p =
0.062$ at $h = 0$ to $p = 0.055$ at $h = 20$) and weakens at longer
horizons ($p = 0.169$ at $h = 60$). Given that the pooled Moderate
AI differential in the preferred specification is itself not
statistically distinguishable from zero at any key horizon, this
asymmetry is best read as a statistical curiosity rather than a
substantive finding about Moderate AI firms.

Two caveats are important. First, splitting the shock by sign halves
the identifying variation per coefficient and the direction-specific
standard errors are roughly double those of the pooled headline
specification (at $h = 60$, the High AI \texttt{path\_tight} standard
error of $4.99$~pp compares to $2.53$~pp in the preferred
specification). None of the direction-specific High AI coefficients
individually cross the $5\%$ significance threshold at the key
horizons, even though their point estimates are economically large.
The substantive content of this section is the qualitative
consistency of the sign and magnitude pattern, not a precise
inference about either direction in isolation. Second, the symmetry
test is a joint restriction that can fail to reject either because
the true response is symmetric or because the data are not powerful
enough to detect departures from symmetry; the latter cannot be
ruled out at this sample size.

With those caveats, the qualitative reading is that the long-horizon
negative differential for High AI firms documented in
\cref{ch:results} is not driven by tightening surprises alone. Point
estimates indicate a comparable positive response to easing
surprises, and a formal symmetry test does not reject the null of
equal-magnitude opposite-direction responses. The result is
qualitatively consistent with a linear, symmetric discount-rate
channel; the data are not powerful enough to detect any non-linear
departure if one is present.
